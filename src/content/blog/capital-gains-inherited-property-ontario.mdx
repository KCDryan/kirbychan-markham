Capital gains on inherited property in Ontario are usually settled on the final tax return of the person who died, not by the family member who inherits. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) treats the owner as having sold everything they owned just before death, at fair market value. Whatever tax that deemed sale creates is paid out of the estate before the beneficiaries receive their shares.

For most families the asset that matters is a house. Whether it was the parent's own home, a rental or a cottage changes the answer a great deal. So does who inherits it and how long the estate holds it before it sells.

This post explains how the CRA rules work for a home, using the CRA's own guidance for people who are filing for someone who died. It is general information, not tax or legal advice. The estate trustee should work with an accountant from the start.

## The short answer

- **At death:** the owner is considered to have sold their property at fair market value immediately before death. The CRA calls this a deemed disposition. Any capital gain goes on the final return.
- **The family home:** a home that was the principal residence for every year it was owned can usually be fully sheltered, but it must still be designated on Form T1255.
- **A spouse inherits:** property left to a spouse or common-law partner can roll over with no gain until the spouse sells.
- **After death:** if the estate or a beneficiary later sells for more than the value at death, that extra gain is taxed to whoever sold it.

## How capital gains on inherited property in Ontario are taxed

The CRA's page for people preparing a deceased person's returns says the person "is considered to have sold all their property just prior to death." The proceeds of that deemed sale are the fair market value of each capital property on the date of death.

The capital gain is that value minus the adjusted cost base, which the CRA's capital gains guide describes as "usually the cost of a property plus any expenses to acquire it, such as commissions and legal fees." Repairs and maintenance cannot be added to the cost base.

Only part of a capital gain is taxable. The CRA's guide for the 2025 tax year says "the inclusion rate for 2025 is 50%." Your accountant will confirm the rate for the year of death, because it is set by federal law and can change.

There are two points families often miss:

1. **The heir does not report the deemed sale.** The gain goes on the deceased person's final return and the legal representative pays the tax from the estate.
2. **The heir starts at the value at death.** The CRA's guide says that when you inherit, "the property's cost to you is equal to the deemed proceeds of disposition" used for the person who died.

## When the house was the family home

Most Markham estates include a house the parent lived in. The principal residence exemption can remove the tax on the gain for the years the home qualified.

The CRA says a home can qualify for a year if you, your spouse or common-law partner or your children lived in it at some time during the year. The CRA's principal residence folio adds that living in a home even for a short period in a year can be enough. Only one home can be designated as the family's principal residence for each year from 1982 on.

The step that trips people up is the paperwork. The CRA says that "even where the entire gain is exempted by a principal residence designation," the legal representative must still designate the property on the final return by completing:

- Schedule 3, Capital Gains or Losses
- Form T1255, Designation of a Property as a Principal Residence by the Legal Representative of a Deceased Individual

The designation only covers the years up to death. If your parent moved to long-term care or a retirement home years before they died, the years after the move may not all qualify. Our post on the [principal residence exemption](/blog/principal-residence-exemption-downsizing/) walks through the CRA formula with an example.

## When a spouse or common-law partner inherits

The CRA says property left to a spouse, a common-law partner or a spousal or common-law partner trust may not result in a capital gain on the final return. The property must vest in the spouse or trust no later than 36 months after the date of death.

The gain is postponed until the spouse sells or is deemed to sell the property. For a principal residence that passes to a spouse, the CRA says a designation for the period before death is not required on the final return.

The legal representative can elect out of the rollover on the final return, property by property. The property is then treated as sold at fair market value. An accountant may suggest this when the person who died has losses or unused amounts that could absorb the gain. That is a decision for the accountant, not something to guess at.

## A rental, a cottage or a second property

A property that was not the principal residence for every year has a taxable gain at death for the years it did not qualify. That covers a rental condo, a cottage the family did not designate or a house the parent moved out of long ago.

A rental adds another layer. The CRA says the deemed disposition of depreciable property may also lead to a recapture of capital cost allowance (CCA) or a terminal loss. Recapture on a rental is reported on Form T776. If your parent claimed CCA on a rental over the years, expect that to come back into income on the final return.

Here is a simple illustration with round numbers. It is not based on any real property.

| Step | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Fair market value of a rental condo at date of death | $700,000 |
| Adjusted cost base (purchase price plus buying costs) | $400,000 |
| Capital gain on the final return | $300,000 |
| Taxable portion at a 50% inclusion rate | $150,000 |

The $150,000 is reported on the final return along with the rest of that year's income. Any CCA recapture would be added on top. The actual tax depends on everything else on the return, so only an accountant can give the real figure.

## If the estate sells the house after death

Estates rarely sell on the date of death. A probate application, clearing the house and preparing it for the market all take time. Our guide to [selling an estate home in Markham](/selling-an-estate-home-markham/) and our post on [selling a house in probate in Ontario](/blog/selling-house-in-probate-ontario/) explain those steps.

Meanwhile the value can move. When the estate sells, it is the estate's own disposition. The CRA's T3 Trust Guide says a trust needs Schedule 1 when it had dispositions of capital property in the year and that a trust's taxable capital gain is generally one half of the gain. The starting point is the value used on the final return. The commission and legal fees on the sale count as outlays and expenses that reduce the gain.

The principal residence exemption is harder to claim for this period. An estate is a type of personal trust. The CRA folio says that for tax years after 2016 a personal trust can only designate a principal residence if it falls into one of a short list of categories. They include certain spousal, alter ego and qualified disability trusts and some trusts for a minor child whose parent has died. Ask your accountant early whether the estate fits, because the answer may affect how long the estate should hold the house.

## If a beneficiary keeps the house

A beneficiary who takes the house rather than the cash from a sale starts with a cost equal to the value at death. The CRA guide also tells taxpayers to "keep a record of the fair market value of the property on the date you inherit it." Ask your accountant how that value should be documented, since it will matter years later when the house is sold.

From then on the normal rules apply to the beneficiary. If they move in, the home can become their principal residence for the years it qualifies. If they rent it out or leave it empty, a later gain over the value at death will be theirs to report.

## What an estate trustee can do now

1. Find the original purchase price and the dates the home was lived in, for every property the person owned.
2. Get a record of each property's fair market value at the date of death. An accountant can tell you what form it should take.
3. Ask the accountant to prepare the final return with Schedule 3 and Form T1255 for the home.
4. Decide with the accountant whether the estate should sell or transfer the house and when.
5. Apply for a CRA clearance certificate on Form TX19 once the returns are filed and assessed, before distributing the estate.

The CRA says a legal representative who distributes assets without a clearance certificate can be held personally liable for unpaid tax, up to the value of what they distributed.
