## The short answer

Conditions in an offer in Ontario let a buyer make the purchase depend on something that has not happened yet. The three a first-time buyer in Markham meets most often are financing, a home inspection and, for a resale condo, a review of the status certificate. CMHC explains that an offer can be "conditional on something else, such as you being approved for a mortgage or having a home inspection done on the house." If any of the conditions are not met, CMHC says, "you can change or cancel the offer, even if the seller has already accepted it."

The Real Estate Council of Ontario (RECO) gives buyers the same advice in its buyer's checklist: if possible, make your offer conditional on mortgage financing, a home inspection, the sale of your existing home or other factors that are important to you. It says these conditions "can provide you with additional assurances and protection."

The trade off is that a seller may prefer an offer with fewer conditions. This post explains what each condition protects, what the official sources say about it and what to weigh before you leave one out.

## How conditions in an offer in Ontario work

An offer is more than a price. CMHC says an offer usually includes the price, the amount of the deposit, the date when the offer is no longer valid, a request for a current land survey and "any other conditions that go with the offer, including property inspection and approval of mortgage financing."

CMHC is direct about what happens once a seller signs: "If the offer is accepted by the seller, it is legally binding." That is why conditions matter. They are the part of a binding agreement that still leaves you room to step back if a specific thing goes wrong.

| Condition | What it protects you from | Who you will be dealing with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Financing | Not getting the mortgage you need | Your lender or mortgage broker |
| Home inspection | Defects you could not see on a showing | A home inspector |
| Status certificate review | Problems with the condo corporation's finances or rules | Your lawyer |
| Sale of your existing home | Owning two homes at once | Your listing agent and lawyer |

The exact wording and the deadline for each condition are written into the agreement. Ask your lawyer to review it. CMHC suggests talking to your lawyer before you make an offer. It also says to be sure you are "prepared to live with everything that is included in your offer before you put it in writing."

## The financing condition

Many first-time buyers assume a pre-approval settles the mortgage question. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) says otherwise. A pre-approval estimates the maximum mortgage and rate a lender may offer, but "this process does not guarantee your approval for a mortgage."

FCAC gives two reasons a pre-approved buyer can still be turned down:

- "Before a lender approves your loan, they'll verify that the property you want meets certain standards." Those standards vary from lender to lender.
- "A lender could refuse you for a mortgage even if you've been preapproved."

CMHC makes the same point from the other side. Even with a pre-approved mortgage certificate, you must "meet with your bank or credit union during the conditional offer period" to get final approval. A financing condition gives you the time to do that before the deal is firm.

A pre-approval does have value. FCAC says lenders may lock in an interest rate for 60 to 130 days depending on the lender. ## The home inspection condition

RECO calls the home inspection condition "one of the most common conditions in an offer to purchase a property." It explains why: the person selling the home may not be aware of property defects. The seller property information statement (SPIS) is based only on their personal knowledge. Your own agent may spot visually obvious defects, but RECO notes that underlying problems can exist.

Ontario's guidance for buyers adds two points. A home inspection "is not a pass or fail test" and does not assess whether a home meets zoning or building codes. And if the seller offers an existing inspection report, Ontario says buyers should have their own home inspection done too. It also suggests you attend the inspection.

If the inspection turns something up, CMHC says you and your agent will need to discuss whether the condition of the home warrants withdrawing your offer or how the repairs might affect the price. RECO's checklist adds that you should be clear if certain renovations or repairs need to be completed before closing. ## The status certificate condition for condos

On a resale condo, the status certificate is the document that tells you about the condo corporation. CMHC says the certificate "should be included as a condition in the Offer to Purchase," and its condominium guide goes further, saying you should make any offer to purchase "conditional on a satisfactory review of these documents."

The Condominium Authority of Ontario (CAO) sets out the rules:

- corporations must provide a status certificate within 10 days
- they can charge up to $100, including all applicable taxes
- the package may include the declaration, by-laws and rules, the budget and audited financial statements, reserve fund information, the unit's common expenses and any arrears, special assessments, insurance certificates and any outstanding legal judgments or litigation

CAO says buyers should review these documents with their legal counsel. It adds that what a certificate includes or leaves out "will bind the corporation." Ontario's buying guidance also notes that the buyer may request and pay for the status certificate. ## The sale of your existing home and other conditions

RECO lists the sale of your existing home as a condition to consider. A first-time buyer will rarely need it, but a buyer who already owns uses it so the purchase does not go ahead until the current home has sold.

RECO also lists "other factors that are important to you." The details depend on the property. CMHC lists a request for a current land survey as a normal part of an offer. The point is to name in writing anything the purchase depends on, rather than assuming it will work out.

## Waiving conditions in a bidding war

If you find yourself up against other offers, RECO is frank about the pressure: "When there are competing offers it can be tempting to waive your conditions (such as a home inspection)." Its advice is to "think twice before doing this."

RECO suggests setting ground rules in advance about what you want from a home, what you are willing to spend and what conditions must be met, then sticking to them. If you choose to firm up without a financing condition, you are relying on the mortgage being approved after you are already committed. If you skip the inspection, you are relying on what you could see. ## Before you write a conditional offer

1. Get a pre-approval and ask your lender what it needs from you and the property before final approval.
2. Decide which conditions you will not give up, before you see the house you want.
3. Book an inspector you can reach quickly, so the inspection fits inside the condition period.
4. For a condo, arrange for your lawyer to review the status certificate.
5. Ask your lawyer to read the offer, including every condition and its deadline, before you sign.
6. Keep your contingency plan in writing in case the dates do not line up.
