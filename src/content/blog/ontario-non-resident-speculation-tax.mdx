## The short answer

The Ontario non-resident speculation tax (NRST) is a 25% provincial tax on residential property bought by a foreign national, a foreign corporation or a taxable trustee. It applies anywhere in Ontario, so it applies in Markham. It is charged on the value of the consideration, which for most purchases is the price. It is paid on top of the regular Ontario land transfer tax.

Three things matter most for a buyer who is not yet a Canadian citizen or permanent resident. Check whether you fit one of the exemptions before you sign. Know that the tax is paid at closing even if you expect a rebate later. Check the separate federal ban on foreign buyers, which is a different law with different exceptions.

This post explains how each piece works, using the Ontario Ministry of Finance pages and CMHC's page on the federal Act. It is general information and not tax or legal advice. A real estate lawyer should confirm your position on your own facts before you make an offer.

## Who pays the Ontario non-resident speculation tax?

Ontario says the tax applies when an interest in residential property is bought by any of the following:

- **Foreign nationals.** Individuals who are not Canadian citizens, permanent residents of Canada or registered as an Indian under the Indian Act.
- **Foreign corporations.** A corporation not incorporated in Canada. The definition also catches some Canadian corporations that are not listed on a Canadian stock exchange and are controlled by foreign entities.
- **Taxable trustees.** A trust with at least one trustee who is a foreign entity or a trust whose beneficiaries include a foreign entity. Mutual fund trusts, real estate investment trusts and SIFT trusts are excluded.

The test is your status on the day the transaction closes. Ontario says that if you have applied to become a permanent resident but have not obtained that status by closing, you must pay the tax unless you qualify for an exemption.

### What property it covers

The tax applies to what the law calls designated land: land that contains at least one and not more than six single family residences. In practice that covers the homes most people buy in Markham, including detached and semi-detached houses, townhouses and condominium units.

Since March 27, 2024, parking units and storage units in a condominium are also designated land. Buying one on its own is subject to the tax.

Ontario says the tax does not apply to land containing multi-residential rental buildings with more than six units. It also does not apply to agricultural, commercial or industrial land.

## How the tax is calculated, with an example

The tax is 25% of the value of the consideration. Ontario's land transfer tax page says the value of the consideration includes the purchase price, liabilities assumed, benefits conferred, soft costs and the cost of upgrades.

It is not split by ownership share. Ontario gives an example of three buyers taking 33%, 33% and 34% interests in a property worth $1,500,000, where only the buyer with 34% is a foreign national. The tax is charged on the full $1,500,000, so $375,000 is payable. Each transferee is liable, so if the foreign buyer does not pay, the others must.

Here is what that looks like alongside the regular land transfer tax on an illustrative $1,500,000 Markham purchase. The land transfer tax figure uses the provincial rates in force on ontario.ca. Markham has no municipal land transfer tax of its own.

| Illustrative example, $1,500,000 purchase | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Ontario land transfer tax | $26,475 |
| Ontario non-resident speculation tax at 25% | $375,000 |
| Total provincial tax at registration | $401,475 |

The land transfer tax line is $275 on the first $55,000, $1,950 on the portion up to $250,000, $2,250 on the portion up to $400,000 and $22,000 at 2% on the remaining $1,100,000. ### How the rate changed since 2017

Agreements signed years ago can still be closing, especially on new construction, so the history matters. Ontario's transitional provisions page sets it out.

| Agreement of purchase and sale signed | Rate | Where it applied |
| --- | --- | --- |
| April 21, 2017 to March 29, 2022 | 15% | Greater Golden Horseshoe, which includes York Region |
| March 30, 2022 to October 24, 2022 | 20% | Anywhere in Ontario |
| From October 25, 2022 | 25% | Anywhere in Ontario |

Ontario warns that the older rates hold only in limited cases. If the land goes to someone other than the purchaser or assignee or their spouse, even through a direction re title, the 25% rate applies. Your lawyer will check this against your agreement.

## Exemptions from the tax

Ontario lists exemptions for registered transfers to three groups of foreign nationals, with conditions attached to each:

1. **Ontario Immigrant Nominee Program nominees.** You must be a nominee at the time of the purchase. Before your nominee certificate expires, you must have applied for permanent residence or certify that you will apply. Ontario says becoming a nominee after closing does not qualify you retroactively and no refund is paid.
2. **Protected persons.** A foreign national on whom refugee protection has been conferred under section 95 of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act.
3. **Spouses.** A foreign national who is the spouse of a Canadian citizen, permanent resident, nominee or protected person, where each spouse is named on the transfer. Spouse includes married partners, partners who have lived together continuously for at least three years and partners in a relationship of some permanence who are the parents of a child. You must be spouses on the closing date.

Every exemption requires all transferees to certify that they will occupy the home as their principal residence within 60 days after the transfer is registered. Exemptions do not apply when a taxable trustee is a transferee.

## The permanent resident rebate and other refunds

Ontario lists two rebates now available. For a home buyer, the relevant one is the permanent resident rebate. If it is approved, Ontario says the entire tax paid is rebated. The conditions include:

- becoming a permanent resident of Canada within four years of the purchase
- holding the property alone or with your spouse only
- occupying the property as your principal residence

The deadline is tight. Ontario says the application must be received within 180 days of becoming a permanent resident. It warns that waiting for your permanent resident card can make you miss it. Only one spouse needs to become a permanent resident for the rebate to apply. The second rebate covers land reclassified for industrial use, which is outside what most Markham home buyers face.

Two older rebates no longer exist. Ontario says the transitional rebates for international students and for foreign nationals working in Ontario applied only to agreements made on or before March 29, 2022. Their application deadline of March 31, 2025 has passed. Many websites still describe them as available.

## When the tax is paid

Ontario says the tax is generally payable when the transaction closes and the transfer is registered on title. Your lawyer declares whether the tax is payable when registering the transfer electronically. The tax is collected then along with the land transfer tax. Our post on [what happens on closing day](/blog/closing-day-ontario-markham/) explains how those funds move through your lawyer.

Ontario is clear that the tax is paid first and rebated later. Buyers who fail to pay at registration may be assessed tax, penalty and interest, even if they turn out to be eligible for a rebate. Budget the full 25% in your closing funds unless your lawyer has confirmed an exemption.

## The federal ban on foreign buyers

Separately from Ontario's tax, the federal Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act restricts who can buy residential property in Canada. CMHC says the federal government announced on February 4, 2024 that it intended to extend the ban to January 1, 2027. Check its status at the time you buy, because that date is close.

According to CMHC, the federal Act covers buildings with three dwelling units or less, including semi-detached houses and condominium units. It says non-Canadians can buy residential property located outside census metropolitan areas and census agglomerations. Have your lawyer confirm how that applies to the address you are considering. CMHC lists exceptions for some students, some work permit holders, refugees and protected persons and non-Canadians buying with a Canadian spouse, each with conditions. It says a buyer who breaks the ban can be fined up to $10,000 and a court can order the sale of the property.

The two laws do not line up. Ontario says people who are subject to an exception under the federal ban may not be exempt from the provincial tax. A buyer can be allowed to buy federally and still owe 25% provincially.

## Before you make an offer

If you are moving to Markham and are not yet a citizen or permanent resident, work through these steps before you sign anything:

1. Confirm your own status and your spouse's status on the expected closing date.
2. Ask a real estate lawyer whether an exemption applies and what you will need to certify.
3. Check the federal ban and its exceptions as they stand on the day you sign.
4. Budget the full tax in your closing funds if no exemption applies, plus the regular land transfer tax.
5. If you expect permanent residence within four years, diarize the 180-day rebate deadline from the day you receive it.
