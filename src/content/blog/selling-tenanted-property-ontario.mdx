## The short answer

Selling a tenanted property in Ontario is allowed, but the sale does not end the tenancy. Under the Residential Tenancies Act (RTA), the buyer steps into your shoes as landlord. The tenant can only be required to leave in the ways the Act allows.

That leaves two routes. You can sell with the tenant staying, which suits a buyer who wants a rental. Or, if the buyer or a close family member intends to move in, you can give the tenant an N12 notice on the buyer's behalf once you have an agreement of purchase and sale. That notice comes with strict timing, a compensation payment from you and real risk if the move-in does not happen.

This post walks through both routes, the rules for showings and what the Canada Revenue Agency expects afterwards. It is general information from the RTA, the Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB) and the CRA, not legal or tax advice. Talk to a lawyer or paralegal before you serve any notice.

## Does the lease survive the sale?

Yes. The RTA defines a landlord to include the landlord's "successors in title," so a buyer becomes the tenant's landlord on closing. Section 18 says covenants related to a rental unit "run with the land," which means the lease terms bind the new owner too.

The Act also says a tenancy "may be terminated only in accordance with this Act," and a landlord cannot recover possession unless the tenant has moved out or the LTB has made an eviction order. Ontario's guide for renters puts it plainly: even with written notice, a tenant does not have to move out until the landlord applies for and receives an eviction order.

A last month's rent deposit also carries over. The RTA says a new landlord cannot require a deposit from a tenant who already paid one to the prior landlord. The deposit must be applied to the last month's rent. Your lawyer will deal with it in the statement of adjustments on closing.

## Selling a tenanted property in Ontario to a buyer who will move in

Section 49 of the RTA lets a seller give notice on behalf of a buyer who wants the home to live in. Two situations qualify:

- **A residential complex with no more than three residential units,** such as a detached house with a basement unit.
- **A condominium unit** that is a rental unit, regardless of how many units the building has.

In both cases you must already have an agreement of purchase and sale. The buyer must require the unit in good faith for residential occupation by the buyer, the buyer's spouse, a child or parent of either of them or a person who provides or will provide care services to the buyer.

The LTB's Interpretation Guideline 12 says the Board may refuse an application if it is not reasonably certain the sale will complete. It names a transfer to a family member or a sale for much less than market value as things that may raise questions. It also says the agreement of purchase and sale should go to the tenant and the LTB at least seven days before any hearing.

Unlike a landlord's own-use notice, the N12 form for a purchaser does not require the person moving in to stay for at least one year. Guideline 12 says the RTA does not state that requirement for a purchaser.

## The N12 notice: dates, compensation and the affidavit

The rules are mechanical and an error can mean starting again. These are the main ones:

| Rule | What the RTA or LTB says |
| --- | --- |
| Notice period | At least 60 days after the notice is given |
| Termination date | The last day of a rental period or, for a fixed term, the end of the term |
| Delivery | Not by posting on the door. Add one business day for courier or five days for mail |
| Compensation | One month's rent or another rental unit acceptable to the tenant |
| Who pays | The seller who gave the notice, not the buyer |
| When | No later than the termination date in the notice |
| Affidavit | Sworn by the person who will live there, filed with the application |

The compensation rule deserves attention. Since September 21, 2026, a landlord giving notice for their own use can avoid compensation by giving at least 120 days' notice. That exception is written for section 48 notices only. The N12 form says compensation must be paid when the notice is given on behalf of a buyer.

If the tenant does not leave, you can apply to the LTB for an eviction order right after giving the notice and no later than 30 days after the termination date. The Board will not accept the application without the affidavit from the person who needs the unit. It cannot issue an eviction order until the compensation has been paid. The tenant can also leave earlier than the termination date by giving at least 10 days' notice on Form N9.

### If the tenancy should end by agreement

A tenant and landlord can agree to end a tenancy at any time, even in the middle of a lease. The LTB says it is best to put the agreement in writing. Its Form N11 is designed for this. If the tenant then does not leave, the landlord can apply to the Board no later than 30 days after the termination date in the agreement.

An agreement must be genuine. The RTA makes it an offence to harass or coerce a tenant into leaving.

## Showings while the tenant lives there

You can show the home to buyers. The RTA allows entry "to allow a potential purchaser to view the rental unit" with written notice given at least 24 hours before the entry. The notice must state the reason, the day and a time of entry between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. A real estate agent registered under the Trust in Real Estate Services Act can enter with the landlord's written authorization.

The rule that allows entry without notice to show a unit applies to prospective tenants, not buyers. Entry at other times needs the tenant's consent at the time. In practice, a sale with a tenant goes better when the tenant knows the showing schedule early. ## Bad faith claims and fines

A notice given for a buyer who never moves in can come back on both seller and buyer. Under section 57, a former tenant has one year after moving out to apply if the notice was given in bad faith and no qualifying person moved in within a reasonable time. The Board can order remedies including general compensation of up to 12 months of the last rent charged, moving and storage costs and an administrative fine.

Guideline 12 says the Board can order the buyer to pay remedies too and that the buyer should be named as a respondent along with the seller. Separately, it is an offence to obtain possession by giving a notice in bad faith or without paying the required compensation. Ontario lists maximum fines of $100,000 for an individual and $500,000 for a corporation.

## Taxes after the sale of a rental

The CRA's rental income guide says that if you sell a rental property for more than it cost, you may have a capital gain, reported on Schedule 3. If you claimed capital cost allowance, you may also have a recapture when the sale proceeds exceed the undepreciated capital cost of the class. The guide also explains how legal fees and commissions are accounted for when you report the sale.

If the home was your principal residence for some years and rented for others, the CRA's change in use rules apply. Our post on the [principal residence exemption](/blog/principal-residence-exemption-downsizing/) explains how that exemption works. An accountant should look at your own numbers before you list.

## A checklist before you list a rented home

1. Read the lease and note the rent, the term and any last month's rent deposit.
2. Decide whether you will market the home to investors with the tenant staying or to buyers who will move in.
3. Tell the tenant early and agree a showing routine with 24 hours' written notice.
4. If the buyer will move in, get legal advice before serving any N12 and diarize the 60 day and compensation deadlines.
5. Ask your accountant about capital gains and recapture before you set a price.
