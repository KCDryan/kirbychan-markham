## The short answer

Selling a house with an open permit in Markham can be done, but it goes more smoothly when the seller finds the permit first. An open permit is the everyday term for a building permit the City issued where the required inspections were never finished. The work may be perfectly sound. The file is simply not complete.

A buyer's lawyer may ask about permits during a sale. If an unfinished permit turns up a week before closing, you are negotiating under pressure. If you find it before you list, you have time to book inspections and collect paperwork.

This post uses City of Markham pages to explain how to check your address, what the City's 2026 fee schedule lists and where work with no permit at all fits in. It is general information, not legal advice. Your real estate lawyer should review the specifics of your property.

## What an open permit means on a Markham home

The City of Markham says you need a building permit before starting any construction, demolition or renovation project, with a short list of exceptions. Getting the permit is only the first half. The City also says all housing projects, including additions and renovations, must be inspected by City building officials.

Ontario's [citizen's guide to building permits](https://www.ontario.ca/document/citizens-guide-land-use-planning/building-permits) puts the job of calling for those inspections on the permit holder. It says it is the permit holder's responsibility to contact the municipality when the project reaches each stage set out in the Building Code.

That is how permits end up open. A contractor finishes the basement, the owner is happy and nobody books the last inspection. Years later the house goes up for sale with a permit still sitting unfinished on the City's file. The City's pages do not define "open permit". It is the term agents and lawyers use for this situation.

Two cases are often confused:

| Situation | What it means | Who to speak to first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open permit | A permit was issued but the inspections were not completed | Building Standards, to book the outstanding inspections |
| No permit | Work that needed a permit was done without one | Your real estate lawyer, then Building Standards about applying |

## Which renovations need a building permit in Markham

Sellers are sometimes surprised by what counts. The City's [building permit process page](https://www.markham.ca/economic-development-business/building-permits/building-permit-process) lists projects that need a permit, including:

- any addition to an existing building, whatever its size
- raised decks more than 24 inches above existing grade at any point
- detached or attached garages and carports
- adding, removing or altering a structural wall or column
- adding, removing or altering a non-loadbearing partition
- installing a basement entrance
- constructing separate rooms in a basement
- installing an accessory apartment
- installing any new plumbing piping
- installing a fireplace or wood stove
- replacing an exterior wall or cladding

The City adds that you need a permit whether the work is on the interior or the exterior of the dwelling. Doing the work inside the house does not create an exemption.

The same page lists housing projects that do not need a building permit. Examples are replacing roofing or shingles, replacing windows or doors with no change to the size of the rough opening, kitchen or bathroom cupboards, replacing a furnace, replacing plumbing fixtures or water heaters and decks less than 600 millimetres above existing grade at all locations.

The City says its lists are for general use and do not cover every situation. For advice on a specific project it gives the number for an Applications Administrator, 905-475-4858. Zoning is a separate question from the permit. Our post on a [minor variance in Markham for a deck, addition or garage](/blog/minor-variance-markham-deck-addition/) covers that side.

## How to check for open permits on a Markham address

Start with your own files, then go to the City. A practical order:

1. List every renovation done while you owned the home: basement finishing, decks, additions, wall removals, new bathrooms and side or basement entrances.
2. Find the paperwork for each one. Look for the permit, the approved drawings, inspection emails and the contractor's invoices.
3. Search the address on the City's ePLAN public search. The City says the search can be used to verify whether a specific address has applied for or obtained a permit.
4. If something is unclear, submit a Building Standards service request. The City says requests for information, certificates and changes to permits are now submitted through the ePLAN portal. Email and website form submissions are no longer accepted.
5. Share what you find with your real estate lawyer and your listing agent before the listing goes live.

The City's building permit pages name a Property Compliance Report, a Certificate of Completion and access to permit drawings among those service requests. The pages do not describe exactly what each document confirms. Ask Building Standards what a document will and will not say before you promise it to a buyer.

If you inherited the home or are selling for a parent, the renovation history may be a mystery. The ePLAN search is the place to start. Our guide to [selling a parent's house in Markham](/blog/selling-a-parents-house-markham/) covers who has authority to sign and make these requests.

## What the City's 2026 fee schedule lists

The City's [building permit fee schedule](https://www.markham.ca/economic-development-business/building-permits/fee-schedule) summarizes Schedule A of Building By-law 2025-121. The City says it applies to all applications submitted after December 31, 2025. These are the lines a seller is most likely to meet:

| Item on the 2026 schedule | Fee |
| --- | --- |
| Certificate of Completion | $210 |
| Compliance Letter | $210 |
| Additional inspection due to work not sufficiently complete | $210 |
| Additional inspection due to a non-remedied infraction | $315 |
| Transfer permit to another owner | $315 |
| Suspended or incomplete permit, housing (annual charge) | $420 |
| Zoning search | $131 |
| Pre-application review for a new permit | $100 |
| Minimum permit fee, residential construction | $151 |

The schedule also lists a fee to extend a permit's expiry date past 12 months: two times the permit fee, to a maximum of $3,150. Which lines apply depends on the age and state of your permit, so treat the table as a guide and confirm the amounts with Building Standards. The City publishes a new schedule each year. The 2025 and 2024 tabs on the same page show lower figures for several of these lines.

Inspections are booked through the City's Inspections Portal. The City says confirmation of a scheduled inspection is emailed to the owner listed on the building permit. If the listed owner is a previous owner or a parent, raise that with Building Standards early.

## What if the work never had a permit

This is the harder case. The City's Building Investigations page gives "construction started without a permit" as an example of a Building Code violation. Its Building Investigation Request page says that where an inspector finds construction in violation of the Building Code Act, the property owner is issued an Order to Comply.

The 2026 fee schedule shows what orders can cost:

| Item on the 2026 schedule | Fee |
| --- | --- |
| Order to Comply | $525 |
| Stop Work Order | $1,575 |
| Order to Comply or Stop Work Order issued prior to building permit | 50 percent of the permit fee, maximum $5,000 for individuals |
| Registration of an Order on Title | $525 |

Ontario's guide adds that an individual found guilty of an offence under the Building Code Act, such as building without a permit, can be fined up to $50,000 for a first offence. It also says failing to comply with an order from the municipal building department is an offence.

None of this tells you what to do about a particular basement or deck. That decision involves your legal obligations to a buyer, the cost of opening finished walls for inspection and your timeline. Take it to your real estate lawyer before you contact the City. Our post on [seller disclosure in Ontario](/blog/seller-disclosure-ontario/) explains the general rules on what a seller must say.

A new permit application also takes time. The City says it can take anywhere from 10 to 40 days to get a building permit. Its target for the first review of a complete application for a house or other small project is 10 business days and 20 for an incomplete one. Applications go through ePLAN with a non-refundable $100 pre-application fee.

## Illustrative example: a finished basement in Unionville

This example is illustrative only. The details are invented for explanation and are not a real property or a real file.

Suppose a couple in Unionville is downsizing from a detached home they have owned for 20 years. In 2015 a contractor finished the basement with a bedroom, a bathroom and a recreation room. A permit was taken out, because the project built separate rooms in a basement and added new plumbing piping. The contractor booked the early inspections. Nobody booked the final ones.

Before listing, the couple searches the address on ePLAN and sees the permit. They find the approved drawings in a filing cabinet. They contact Building Standards, book the outstanding inspections through the Inspections Portal and ask whether a Certificate of Completion is available once the inspections pass. If an inspector asks for a correction, there is time to make it before the listing photos are taken.

Now picture the same basement discovered by the buyer's lawyer 10 days before closing. The inspections and any corrections may not fit in the time left. The lawyers then have to negotiate a delay, a price change or some other arrangement. The work is identical in both versions. Only the timing is different.

## Before you list in Unionville, Markham Village or Thornhill

Markham's older areas have had the most time to collect renovations. Homes in [Unionville](/unionville-markham/), [Markham Village](/markham-village-markham/) and Markham-side [Thornhill](/thornhill-markham/) may have passed through several owners, each with their own projects. Newer areas are not exempt. A finished basement or a raised deck in Cornell, Wismer or Berczy Village raises the same question. If the house is in a heritage conservation district, exterior changes have their own approval process, which our guide to [older homes in Markham](/blog/older-homes-markham-what-to-check/) explains.

A short checklist before the sign goes up:

- Search the address on the City's ePLAN public search.
- Match every renovation to a permit and a completed inspection record.
- Book any outstanding inspections and ask about a Certificate of Completion.
- Take any work without a permit to your real estate lawyer first.
- Build the time into your plan. Our [Markham home selling timeline](/blog/selling-a-house-in-markham-timeline/) shows where this step fits.
