## The short answer

Buying a house in Markham Heritage Estates is buying into one of the most unusual streets in the whole of York Region, because every house there was built somewhere else in Markham first, sometimes more than a century and a half ago and was then lifted, moved and restored on a lot the City created for exactly that purpose.

* The City of Markham calls Markham Heritage Estates Canada's first heritage subdivision, a 42 lot subdivision it owns near Highway 48 and 16th Avenue.
* It works as a subdivision of last resort for Markham heritage buildings that cannot be kept on their original sites.
* Every house is designated under the Ontario Heritage Act and carries a heritage easement with the City, both registered on title.
* Exterior changes, including paint colours, fences and additions, need the City's written approval.
* The real work for a buyer is on paper: the designation, the easement, any City mortgage still on title and the running costs of an older house.

What follows is drawn from the City's own page and its February 2021 administrative guidelines for the subdivision. It is general information to help you ask better questions, not legal advice and your lawyer is the person who confirms what is registered on the title of any specific house.

## Why Markham built a neighbourhood from relocated houses

Markham grew fast in the 1980s and the City's guidelines are candid about what that cost: between 1981 and 1988, more than forty structures listed in the Markham Inventory of Historic Buildings were demolished, some of them because they simply could not stay next to new development, road widening and new highways. Moving a threatened house was possible in theory, but buying a lot in the Markham area and paying for the move made it rare in practice.

So in 1988 the municipality created Markham Heritage Estates to close that gap. The City sells the lots below market value to owners of qualifying heritage buildings and the money saved on the lot is the incentive to relocate and restore a house that would otherwise be lost. The City says the subdivision has proved useful where retention on site was not feasible, such as the construction of Highway 407, road widening and stormwater management facilities.

The City also sees a wider payoff. Its page says that, together with stronger policies for keeping buildings within new developments, Markham Heritage Estates has virtually eliminated the threat of losing significant heritage buildings in Markham and that the accurate restorations show developers and the public what restoring a heritage building can achieve.

### Which Markham houses qualified

Not every old house could move in. The guidelines say a building must be located within the City of Markham, be of significant merit as recommended by Markham Council and be the subject of a verifiable threat on its existing site. The City expects every effort to keep a heritage building where it was first built, so relocation is the option that is left when the alternatives have run out.

That is why the City describes the finished community as a legacy of once threatened, predominantly rural heritage dwellings indigenous to Markham. The Galloway House on David Gohn Circle is a good example: the City's own page shows the house, built around 1858, on the move in 1995, alongside the James Thomas House, built around 1856.

## What makes a house in Markham Heritage Estates different on paper

From the street, a house in Markham Heritage Estates reads as a beautifully restored older home. The guidelines say each relocated building is restored to its original condition, that new additions usually sit at the rear and are compatible with the heritage character and that garages are detached and placed behind the house. The differences that shape your ownership, though, sit in the documents registered on title.

| What the City's guidelines describe | What it means for a buyer |
| --- | --- |
| Designation of the exterior and on occasion interior elements under the Ontario Heritage Act, registered on title | The heritage features are protected and changes to them go through the City |
| A heritage easement agreement with the City, registered on title | A legal interest for the City in the property; the guidelines call easements more comprehensive than designation and say a perpetual easement is the most appropriate |
| No external alterations, including colours, without the City's written approval | Even repainting the trim goes through the City before the brushes come out |
| Fences, hedges, gates, accessory buildings and additions need approved plans | Plan sheds, fences and extensions with the City's heritage section from the start |
| A sample site plan clause warning that traffic on 16th Avenue may increase and noise may become a concern | Visit at different times of day if the lot backs toward 16th Avenue |

The guidelines also explain why the easement matters so much: the Ontario Heritage Act on its own does not require an owner of a designated property to keep the building in good condition, insure it or rebuild it after a loss, while an easement agreement can. Ask your lawyer to walk you through what the easement on your particular house requires, because those obligations travel with the property to you.

### The City mortgage on title

Because the lots were sold below market value, the City uses a forgivable mortgage to stop people from flipping them. The guidelines say the original buyer gives the City an interest free second mortgage of 50% of the lot price, registered on title and reduced by 20% a year over five years, then discharged as long as the owner complies with the site plan agreement. The City notes it only affects an owner who sells within those five years and that, in its review, no one has left within the five year period.

For a resale buyer, that turns into one plain question for your lawyer: is any City mortgage still registered on this title and has it been discharged?

## The running costs of an older Markham house

The City is unusually honest with its own applicants about money. The guidelines say a number of Heritage Estates owners found their real costs were much higher than their first budgets and that owners may face higher operating and maintenance costs, such as heating and air conditioning with limited insulation and single pane heritage windows and the ongoing upkeep of wood features like wall cladding, soffits, fascia and porches.

That does not make these houses a poor choice; it makes them a considered one. Ask the seller for utility bills across a full year, ask what maintenance has been done to the wood and windows and when; build a realistic upkeep budget into your plan before you fall in love with the porch. ## Markham Heritage Estates versus Markham's heritage districts

It is easy to blur Markham Heritage Estates with the City's heritage conservation districts and they are not the same thing. Under Part V of the Ontario Heritage Act, Markham has protected four heritage conservation districts: Thornhill, Markham Village, Buttonville and Unionville. In those districts, the protection applies across a whole historic area and the City's heritage permit rules reach every property inside the district boundary.

Markham Heritage Estates is different: it is a single City-created subdivision, every house in it was relocated there and each one is individually protected through its own designation and easement. ## Questions to settle before you make an offer

When a house in Markham Heritage Estates comes up, the buyers who do well are the ones who treat the paperwork with the same care as the floor plan. Before you firm up an offer:

* Ask your lawyer to review the designation by-law, the heritage easement and the site plan agreement registered on title and to confirm whether any City mortgage remains.
* Ask the City of Markham's heritage section what kinds of exterior changes, additions or fences it is likely to approve for that property.
* Ask the City for the property's permit history and our post on [selling or buying a house with an open permit in Markham](/blog/selling-house-open-permit-markham/) explains why that matters.
* Book a home inspection that looks closely at the foundation the house was set on, the windows and the wood heritage features.
* Talk to your lender and your insurer early about the heritage designation and easement, so their questions do not land in the last days before closing.
