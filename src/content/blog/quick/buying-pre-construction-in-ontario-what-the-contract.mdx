## The short answer

* You are signing a builder-drafted contract, not buying a home and the two are governed by very different rules.
* Ontario's 10-day cooling-off period starts when you sign or receive a fully executed copy of the agreement, whichever is later.
* A real estate lawyer should review the agreement during those 10 days, not after, because that is the only window to walk away cleanly.
* Deposits, closing costs, occupancy dates and assignment rights are all set by the builder's paperwork, not by standard market convention.
* Cancelling after the cooling-off period ends is difficult and can put part or all of your deposit at risk.

## Buying Pre-Construction in Ontario Means Signing a Contract First

When you buy a resale condo, you're buying something that exists. You walk through it, you see the finishes, you get an inspection if you want one and on closing day the title transfers. Pre-construction works differently. You're signing an agreement of purchase and sale for a unit that might not be built for another two, four or even six years.

That agreement is drafted by the builder's lawyers, not yours. It sets out the price, the deposit schedule, the estimated occupancy date, the finishes you're entitled to and dozens of clauses about delays, cost adjustments and cancellation rights that almost always favour the builder. Buying pre-construction in Ontario is really an exercise in contract law long before it becomes an exercise in real estate.

This matters because a lot of buyers treat the signing appointment like they'd treat an offer on a resale home: read it quickly, sign where indicated, move on. With pre-construction, that approach can cost you tens of thousands of dollars down the road in unexpected closing adjustments or a development that stalls for years.

## What Ontario's 10-Day Cooling-Off Period Actually Does

Ontario's New Home Construction Licensing Act gives buyers of new condo units a 10 calendar day cooling-off period after signing a purchase agreement or after receiving a copy signed by both parties, whichever comes later. During those 10 days, you can cancel for any reason or no reason at all and the builder must return your deposit in full.

The point of the cooling-off period isn't to give you time to change your mind about the colour of the countertops. It exists because pre-construction agreements are complicated, one-sided by default and impossible to properly understand without legal training. The law assumes, correctly, that most buyers sign first and only start asking questions afterward.

Ten days sounds like plenty of time, but it moves fast once you account for weekends, work schedules and the time it takes a lawyer to actually review a 60-plus page document. If you're seriously considering a project, the smart move is lining up your lawyer before you sign anything, not after.

### What Counts as Cancelling in Time

To cancel within the cooling-off period, you generally need to deliver written notice to the builder (or the builder's lawyer) before the 10 days expire. Verbal notice or telling your sales rep you've changed your mind, usually isn't enough on its own. Your lawyer can prepare and send this notice properly if it comes to that, which is one more reason to have one lined up early rather than scrambling on day nine.

## Why the Cooling-Off Period Is When a Lawyer Should Read the Agreement

A lot of buyers assume their lawyer's job starts near closing, when it's time to review the final statement of adjustments. With pre-construction, that's backwards. The agreement you sign at the sales centre is the document that determines almost everything that happens afterward and once the cooling-off period ends, you're bound by it.

A lawyer experienced in pre-construction deals is looking for things a buyer typically wouldn't catch:

* Development charges, levies and utility hookup fees that can add many thousands of dollars to closing costs, often uncapped or capped at a number far higher than buyers expect.
* Occupancy date language that separates "interim occupancy" from actual closing, sometimes by a year or more, during which you pay occupancy fees but don't own the unit.
* Assignment clauses that restrict or heavily fee-load your ability to sell the contract before closing if your plans change.
* Delay and termination clauses that let the builder push dates or cancel the project under fairly broad conditions.
* Deposit structure and whether it's held in trust as required and how it's insured.

This is precisely the kind of review that has to happen inside the 10-day window. Once it closes, you can still ask a lawyer for advice, but you've lost your ability to walk away without penalty and any changes you want now require the builder's cooperation rather than your legal right to cancel.

## Deposits, Tarion and Where Your Money Actually Sits

Pre-construction condo deposits in Ontario are typically due in installments over the first year or so after signing, often totalling somewhere between fifteen and twenty percent of the purchase price, though the exact structure varies by builder and project. These deposits are generally required to be held in trust and Tarion, Ontario's new home warranty program, provides deposit protection up to certain limits.

The specifics of that protection and how it differs for condo units versus freehold homes, change from time to time, so this is exactly the kind of detail to confirm with your lawyer rather than take from a sales brochure. Don't assume your deposit is automatically fully protected. Ask and get it in writing.

## Pre-Construction vs Resale: What You're Actually Buying

| Factor                  | Pre-Construction Condo                                     | Resale Condo                                                                      |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What you sign           | Builder-drafted purchase agreement for a future unit       | Standard OREA agreement of purchase and sale                                      |
| Cooling-off period      | 10 calendar days under Ontario law                         | None, offer is binding once accepted (subject to conditions)                      |
| Closing costs           | Development charges, levies and adjustments often uncapped | Land transfer tax, legal fees and adjustments are known and calculable in advance |
| Timeline to ownership   | Often two to six years from signing to final closing       | Typically 30 to 90 days from accepted offer                                       |
| Ability to see the unit | Model suite or floor plan only                             | Full in-person viewing and inspection possible                                    |
| Exit before closing     | Assignment sale, if the agreement permits it               | Not applicable, you own the unit outright at closing                              |

## Where Pre-Construction Prices Actually Come From

Pre-construction prices are set by the builder based on projected costs, expected market conditions years out and how quickly they want to sell out a project. Early "VIP" or platinum access pricing is often lower specifically to generate momentum, not necessarily because it reflects fair market value at completion.

This means the price you sign at today has no fixed relationship to what the unit will be worth when it finally closes. Markets can rise or fall over a multi-year build timeline and buyers sometimes close on units worth less than what they agreed to pay or find their financing no longer qualifies at the appraised value. This is a real risk worth discussing with both your lawyer and your mortgage professional before you commit deposit money.

## Assignment Sales: Your Exit Ramp, If the Contract Allows It

Life changes over a two-to-six year building timeline. Buyers sometimes need to sell their contract before the building closes, which is called an assignment. Not every pre-construction agreement allows this and the ones that do often charge a builder consent fee, restrict marketing of the assignment or require the original buyer to close first and rely on a "flip" of the contract shortly before completion.

If there's any chance your circumstances might change before closing, ask specifically about the assignment clause before you sign and have your lawyer explain exactly what it permits. This is not a detail to discover after you've already committed thousands of dollars in deposits.

## What to Do Next

* Before visiting a sales centre, line up a real estate lawyer who specifically handles pre-construction condo deals, not just resale closings.
* If you sign, calendar the 10-day cooling-off deadline immediately and get the agreement to your lawyer within the first day or two, not the last.
* Ask the builder directly about development charge caps, occupancy fee schedules and assignment rights before you sign and get answers in writing where possible.
* Confirm with your lawyer how your deposit is held and what Tarion protection applies to your specific unit and project.
* Talk to your mortgage professional about how a multi-year closing timeline could affect your financing and appraised value at completion.
