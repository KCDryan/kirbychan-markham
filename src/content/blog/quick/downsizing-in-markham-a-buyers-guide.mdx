## The short answer

* Figure out your rough numbers first: what your current home is worth, what you owe and what a smaller place in Markham actually costs today.
* Decide early whether you'll sell before buying or buy before selling, since that choice shapes everything else about the move.
* Condos and townhomes trade yard work for a monthly fee, so compare that fee honestly against what you currently spend on upkeep.
* Start sorting belongings months before listing, because downsizing is as much a physical project as a financial one.
* Get a proper condo status certificate reviewed by a lawyer before committing to any condo purchase.

## Why Markham buyers are downsizing right now

A lot of the downsizing I see in Markham comes from people who bought their home fifteen, twenty or thirty years ago, raised a family in it and now find themselves rattling around three or four bedrooms that don't get used. The house did its job. Now it's mostly extra cleaning, an unused basement and a lawn or driveway that takes more effort every year.

The other driver is equity. Markham detached homes have appreciated significantly over the decades and for a lot of long-time owners, the house is by far their largest asset. Downsizing in Markham lets people unlock some of that value, whether the plan is to fund retirement, help kids with a down payment or simply stop worrying about a big mortgage or big repair bills.

Some people downsize because of stairs, mobility or wanting to be closer to family. Others just want less to manage. Whatever the reason, it helps to be clear about yours, because it changes what "smaller" should actually mean for you.

## What downsizing in Markham actually looks like

Downsizing doesn't have one shape. Some buyers move from a detached house to a condo apartment near Markham's transit and shopping. Others move to a bungalow or bungaloft, which keeps them on one level without giving up a yard entirely. Some stay in Markham to remain close to family and their doctors and others use the move to relocate closer to adult children in another part of the GTA.

### Common downsizing moves in Markham

* Detached house to condo apartment: less space, a monthly fee, but no exterior maintenance.
* Detached house to bungalow or bungaloft: one-level living, often a smaller lot, but bungalows are scarce and priced at a premium.
* Larger detached to smaller detached or semi: keeps a similar lifestyle with less square footage to heat, cool and clean.
* Freehold townhome: a middle ground between a house and a condo, sometimes with a small fee for shared common elements.

None of these is automatically the right answer. A bungalow suits someone who still wants a garden and some independence from shared building rules. A condo suits someone who wants to lock the door and travel without worrying about the property. It's worth being honest with yourself about which lifestyle you actually want rather than which one sounds more appealing on paper.

## Sell first or buy first: the decision that shapes everything

This is usually the first real fork in the road when downsizing in Markham. There's no universally correct order, just trade-offs.

Selling first gives you a firm, known number. You know exactly what you have to work with before you commit to a purchase and you're not carrying two mortgages or two sets of property taxes at once. The trade-off is that you may need to arrange temporary housing or negotiate a longer closing or rent-back period with your buyer so you have time to find your next place.

Buying first means you know exactly where you're going and can move once, but it ties up equity you haven't accessed yet and it means carrying two properties for a period unless you arrange a bridge loan. Bridge financing is common in these situations and your lender or mortgage broker can walk you through the current terms and costs, which change often enough that it's worth confirming them directly rather than relying on a number from a few years ago.

A lot of Markham sellers land on a middle path: list the house with a longer closing date and use that window to shop for and firm up the next place. It takes some coordination, but it avoids both the housing gap and the double-carrying-cost problem.

## What a smaller home actually costs to run

One thing that surprises people about downsizing in Markham is that "smaller" doesn't always mean "cheaper to live in" once you factor in fees. A condo fee typically covers building insurance, common area maintenance, amenities and sometimes heat and water. Depending on the building, that fee can range widely, so ask for the actual current fee and history of increases before assuming it fits your budget.

Compare that fee honestly against what you currently spend keeping up a house: furnace and roof life-cycle costs, lawn care, snow removal, eavestrough cleaning and general repairs. For a lot of people the math works out close to even or even in favour of the condo once you stop doing (or paying for) the physical upkeep yourself.

Property taxes also shift with the assessed value of the new home and they don't always drop proportionally with square footage, especially for bungalows or well-located townhomes. It's worth getting a tax estimate for any specific property you're considering rather than assuming it will simply be a smaller bill.

## Land transfer tax and other closing costs

Whatever you buy next in Ontario, you'll pay provincial land transfer tax, calculated on a sliding scale based on the purchase price. First-time buyers may qualify for a rebate, though most people downsizing have owned before and won't be eligible. If you're moving into the City of Toronto specifically, there's a second, municipal land transfer tax on top of the provincial one, but that doesn't apply within Markham itself.

Add in legal fees, a home inspection if you're buying a house and moving costs and it's worth budgeting a few thousand dollars beyond the purchase price itself. Your lawyer can give you a precise breakdown once you have an accepted offer and it's worth asking for that estimate in writing before you're locked into a closing date.

## What to look for in a condo status certificate

If downsizing in Markham means moving into a condo, the status certificate is one of the most important documents you'll review. In Ontario, buyers or their lawyer can request this package from the condo corporation and it includes the building's financial statements, reserve fund study, current fees, rules and any outstanding legal disputes.

A healthy reserve fund means the building has money set aside for major repairs like roofs, elevators or parking garage work, so you're less likely to face a large special assessment down the road. A thin reserve fund or a big planned increase in fees, is worth knowing about before you commit, not after you've moved in. Have your real estate lawyer review the status certificate within the timeline your agreement allows.

## Sorting through decades of belongings

This part of downsizing often takes longer than the real estate side, so don't leave it to the last few weeks. Start by sorting into simple categories: keep, give to family, sell, donate and discard. Ask family members directly what they actually want rather than assuming, since a lot of well-meant furniture and china gets politely declined.

Many Markham sellers use a short-term storage locker to bridge the gap between selling the house and knowing exactly how much fits in the new place. Others hire a downsizing or senior move management service, which can handle sorting, packing and coordinating donations if the physical work feels like too much on top of everything else. Either way, giving yourself a few months' lead time makes the whole move noticeably calmer.
