## The short answer

* Relocated houses are moved using steel beams, hydraulic jacks and dollies, a process that can keep furniture and even dishes in the cabinets.
* Homes are usually moved to clear land for new development or to save a historic structure from demolition.
* Most moves happen over short distances because overhead utility lines make long-distance moves expensive.
* A relocated home needs a new foundation, updated utility connections and fresh inspections before anyone can live in it again.
* Markham has its own example: Markham Heritage Estates, a City-created subdivision of relocated heritage houses near Highway 48 and 16th Avenue.
* Buying there means checking the heritage designation, the heritage easement and the City's approval rules, not just the usual home inspection.

## Markham Heritage Estates was built from relocated buildings

Most neighbourhoods grow the ordinary way: a developer buys land, a builder puts up houses and the street fills in over a few years. A neighbourhood built from relocated buildings skips that step entirely. The houses were built somewhere else first, sometimes a century or more earlier. They were later picked up and moved in to fill the lots.

Markham has one. The City of Markham describes Markham Heritage Estates as Canada's first heritage subdivision: a 42 lot subdivision owned by the City that works as a subdivision of last resort for heritage buildings in Markham that cannot be kept on their original sites. The City's guidelines place it on a 7 hectare parcel near Highway 48 and 16th Avenue in Markham.

It sounds unusual because it is. Walking down a street like this, you are not looking at houses that were designed for that spot. You are looking at survivors, buildings that were headed for demolition and got a second address instead.

## Why Markham moved heritage houses instead of losing them

In Markham the reason was growth. The City's guidelines say that between 1981 and 1988 more than forty structures on the Markham Inventory of Historic Buildings were demolished, some because they could not be kept on site next to new development, road widening and new highways. Moving them was rarely done because buying a lot in the Markham area and paying for the move cost too much.

In 1988 the municipality created Markham Heritage Estates to close that gap. The City sells the lots below market value to owners of qualifying heritage buildings. The saving on the lot is the incentive to relocate and restore the house. The City says the subdivision has been useful when buildings could not stay on site because of the construction of Highway 407, road widening and stormwater management facilities.

### What qualifies a house for Markham Heritage Estates

The City's guidelines say a building must be located within the City of Markham, be of significant merit as recommended by Markham Council and face a verifiable threat on its existing site. The City requires every effort to keep a heritage building where it was built first. Relocation is approved only when all options for keeping it on site have been exhausted and demolition is imminent.

So the houses did not start out together. They were pulled from different original addresses across Markham, which is the quiet thing that sets these streets apart: each building has two histories, where it was built and where it ended up.

## How do movers actually relocate an entire house?

It is more routine than it sounds, though it looks dramatic from the sidewalk. Crews dig access holes and slide steel beams underneath the structure, then stack cribbing, timber blocks arranged like oversized Lincoln Logs, to support the house as hydraulic jacks lift it clear of its foundation. Once it is high enough, hydraulic dollies with independent axles and brakes go underneath and the whole building is rolled to its new site, sometimes pushed by truck, sometimes along temporary rails for short distances.

The process is gentler on the structure than most people expect. Homeowners in some moves have kept furniture inside and movers describe leaving dishes in the cabinets. Chimneys and other protruding features usually have to come off first and anything overhead along the route, wires, trees, traffic signals, has to be cleared or temporarily removed.

* Jacking: the building is lifted off its foundation on a temporary steel frame.
* Cribbing: timber stacks support the structure at the lifted height.
* Dollies: hydraulic, wheeled supports carry the house along the route.
* Resetting: the house is lowered onto a new foundation at the destination and reconnected to utilities.

## Why these neighbourhoods tend to be small and close together

Distance is the limiting factor in almost every move. Once a relocation stretches more than a few miles, the cost of temporarily taking down overhead utility lines along the route usually makes the project too expensive to pencil out. That is a big part of why a neighbourhood built from relocated buildings tends to be a tight cluster rather than something spread across a city: the houses were moved from nearby lots that were being cleared, not shipped in from across the region.

Markham Heritage Estates fits that pattern. Every house in it had to be located within the City of Markham before the move. The City's guidelines describe the finished community as a legacy of once threatened, predominantly rural heritage dwellings indigenous to Markham.

## What buying in Markham Heritage Estates involves

From the curb, a relocated house usually looks like any other older home. The differences that matter are underneath and on paper. A proper move means a brand new foundation built to current code, full reconnection of plumbing, electrical and other utilities and inspections done after the house is reset, on top of whatever inspection you would normally order as a buyer.

Ask for the move records. A legitimate relocation will have permits, the name of the house-moving company and engineering sign-off on both the lift and the new foundation.

In Markham Heritage Estates the heritage rules matter as much as the structure. The City's guidelines say:

* The exterior of each relocated building is designated under the Ontario Heritage Act, sometimes with interior elements too. The designation is registered on title.
* Each owner enters into a heritage easement agreement with the City, also registered on title. The guidelines describe easements as more comprehensive than designation and say a perpetual easement is the most appropriate.
* No external alteration, including a change of colour, may be made without the City's written approval. Additions, fences and accessory buildings need approval too.
* Original purchasers of a lot take a City second mortgage that is forgiven over five years of living in the house. If they sell, lease or move out before the five years are up, the remaining balance becomes due.

Ask your lawyer to review the designation by-law, the easement and any City mortgage on title before you firm up an offer. The City's heritage section can answer questions about what changes are likely to be approved. Heritage Estates is separate from Markham's four heritage conservation districts in Thornhill, Markham Village, Buttonville and Unionville, where the City's heritage permit rules apply to every property in the district.

### Financing and insurance on a relocated home

Lenders and insurers are not usually spooked by a relocated house, but they will want the paperwork. Expect to be asked for the foundation engineering report, permits from the move itself and proof that utilities were brought up to current code during the reset. Build time into your closing timeline to track this down if the seller has not already gathered it.

## What to do next

If you are curious about a house in Markham Heritage Estates or another relocated home in Markham, start with the property's permit history from the City of Markham building department. Ask whether it shows the move and the new foundation separately from any later renovation permits.

* Ask the listing agent for the name of the house-moving company and any engineering reports from the move.
* Ask the City of Markham heritage section and your lawyer what the designation by-law and heritage easement on title allow.
* Book an inspection that specifically covers the foundation and utility reconnections, not just the standard pre-purchase checklist.
* Talk to your lender early about any extra documentation they want for a relocated structure, so it does not slow down closing.
