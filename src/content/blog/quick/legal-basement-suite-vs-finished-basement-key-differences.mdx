## The short answer

* A kitchen, a bathroom and a separate entrance make a basement look like a suite, but they do not make it legal.
* Zoning has to allow the second unit on that specific lot and Ontario law now allows additional residential units as of right in many cases.
* Building and fire safety standards apply and in Markham the City inspects them.
* In Markham, all two-unit houses must be registered with the City.
* Lenders, insurers and buyers can treat registered and unregistered suites differently, so check before you rely on one.

## Why a Finished Basement Is Not Automatically a Legal Suite

I see this all the time. A seller tells me their basement is "a legal suite" because it has its own kitchen, its own bathroom and a door that goes straight outside. That description tells me the basement is finished and self-contained. It tells me nothing about whether it is legal.

Legal status has nothing to do with how the space looks or how well it was built. It is a set of approvals from your municipality that confirm the unit meets zoning, safety and building requirements and has been inspected. A contractor can do beautiful work and still produce a basement that cannot legally be rented as a separate unit, because the renovation never went through the permit process in the first place.

This matters because the gap between "looks like a suite" and "is a legal basement suite" shows up at the worst possible times, such as during a sale, during a mortgage application or after a fire or a tenant dispute.

## What Zoning Has to Allow

Before you think about permits or inspections, the first question is whether your property can have a second unit. Under Ontario's additional residential unit framework, up to three units are permitted as of right in the primary building. The alternative is up to two units in the primary building and one in an ancillary building such as a garage. The province has said this overrides local zoning bylaws. Other requirements, such as setbacks and building height, can still apply.

If you are buying a home with an existing basement apartment, checking compliance for that specific address is one of the first calls your agent or lawyer should make, well before closing. Our [buyers page](/buyers/) explains how I approach that kind of due diligence.

## Building Code and Fire Safety Requirements

Once zoning allows a second unit, the physical space has to meet the Ontario Building Code and fire safety standards. Markham describes a two-unit house as one where each unit has its own kitchen, bathroom, living, sleeping and eating areas, plus a protected exit pathway. Markham's Building Standards staff check that construction meets the Ontario Building Code and Markham Fire & Emergency Services carry out a separate fire safety inspection.

Ask Building Standards how the code applies to the details of your basement, for example ceiling height, bedroom windows, fire separation between the units, alarms and electrical work. A finished basement with a kitchen and a side door can fall short on any of these and still look perfectly livable day to day. The problem only becomes visible when there is a fire, an insurance question or a buyer's lawyer asking pointed questions.

## Permits, Inspection and Registration: The Part People Skip

In Markham, a building permit is required for new construction of a second suite and all two-unit houses must be registered with the City. The process generally involves:

* Contacting Building Standards (905.475.4870) and getting the permits before the work starts, not after it is finished.
* Passing the building inspections the permit calls for.
* Arranging a fire inspection with Markham Fire & Emergency Services (905.415.7521).
* Registering the two-unit house with the City. The City lists a $260 fire department initial inspection fee and a $246 registration fee. Confirm current fees with the City before you apply.
* Completing the Two-Unit House Declaration Form, which the City points to for suites that predate July 1, 1993.

This is the step that turns "we built it to code" into a legal basement suite the City recognizes. Plenty of basements are built by capable tradespeople who genuinely followed good practice but skipped permits to save time or money. That basement may be safe. It is still not registered until the paperwork exists and Markham states that failing to register may result in court action and fines against the owner.

## Why This Distinction Matters When You Sell

When I list a home with a basement suite, the first question buyers and their agents ask is whether it is registered and legal. An unregistered suite can still appeal to buyers who like extra income and flexible space, but the uncertainty around it can slow a deal down while they try to confirm what you cannot confirm for them.

A documented, legal basement suite is a different conversation. You can show permits, your registration and often a lease. That gives the buyer less to investigate on their own. If you are thinking about selling, a [home valuation](/home-valuation/) conversation is a good time to raise the suite and our [sellers page](/sellers/) covers how I prepare a listing.

## Why This Distinction Matters When You Rent or Finance

If you are renting out an unregistered suite, you are taking on risk that many homeowners underestimate. Beyond the City's enforcement powers, your insurer may ask questions about how the space is used, so speak with your insurance provider before you advertise the unit.

On the financing side, lenders set their own policies on rental income and many want proof that the suite is legal. Policies vary, so this is a conversation to have directly with a mortgage broker rather than assume either way. ## What to Do Next

If you are selling a home with a basement apartment, pull together whatever documentation you have this week, including permits, inspection reports, your registration and your current lease if the suite is rented. If some of that is missing, it is worth finding out now, not after an offer comes in, how to bring the suite into compliance before you list.

If you are buying a home advertised with a basement suite, do not take the listing's word for it. Ask directly whether the suite is registered, request copies of permits and have your lawyer confirm compliance for that address before you firm up the deal.

If you already own a finished basement and are considering renting it out, start with a call to Markham Building Standards to confirm what your project needs, then talk to a contractor familiar with secondary suite requirements before you advertise the unit.
